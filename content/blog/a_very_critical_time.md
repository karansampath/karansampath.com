I've been at Anthropic for over nine months now, and I could hardly have predicted what was to come. I've travelled across the world and worked across GTM, research, and product. Moving to a new city, London, has been wonderful and challenging through it all, and I can hardly think of a more fulfilling year. It's also been one full of learnings, and thoughts of where we have been and where we're going. Here are a few takes on the past and future, which share common themes but may come off as haphazard and disorganized. I apologize in advance :)

1. AI progress this year has happened much faster than I expected before joining. [Mythos](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-5-mythos-5), a few months into joining, was the major update for me, but the field as a whole has progressed light years faster than I expected. 2027 will likely be critical for humanity and the AI community as a whole. I hope we're able to better communicate to the world the importance of the issue here.

2. I'm pleasantly surprised to see that video models have not taken off to the degree I expected. Fundamentally I believe entertainment is a function of human attention, and human attention is going to overwhelmingly prioritize that which is scarce: live events, live shared interactions, even to the extent of shared classes. Consumers are also far more aware and suspicious than I initially supposed. Critically, although AI video models have improved (though to a lesser degree than I previously expected, mainly due to compute redirection), adoption hasn't tracked to the same degree.

3. Shared experience, both at work and at home, will become more important, and I think that's a positive world we will make happen. Terence Tao's [talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M0--ZH1lOzg) on the math community focusing on acceptance and canonicalization in a proof-abundant world struck a chord with me here. Math will set the standard for the rest of the knowledge work domains. At home, we'll further prioritize real-world shared events with other humans, and this dialectical experience will become a core way of defining ourselves.

4. There will be work we do through directing AIs, but at a far higher level — we will reorient our work away from purely directing them. Within that, we continue to derive joy from convincing others to consume our work, since that's a core part of the human experience.

5. I suspect a drop in medium-size companies (10k–50k employees) towards a more bimodal distribution in enterprise sizes. Companies will either run extremely lean on AI, or will lean into the in-person component to the point that it requires large levels of manpower. There will be fewer of the largest-size companies, and I suspect they will serve far more external clients than just their own.

6. I've been surprised by how international AI has become. When I first joined Anthropic, hardly anyone I met in a London pub knew the name (though many had heard of 'AI' in general). Fast forward nine months, and not only do people keenly know of AI and Anthropic, but they also have in-depth opinions that I try to engage with. Living in SF gave me the impression of being light years in the future, but I've actually come to update myself strongly against that. Being able to stay in the real world (read: not SF) while working on frontier AI has become the optimal combination for me.

7. Related to the above, I predict the center of gravity in AI will move out from SF in the future. By center of gravity here I mean the place most keenly pushing AI forward. In particular, I imagine that as AI automates most digital technical work, the core bottleneck will shift to physical infrastructure work and economic diffusion in non-digital use cases. Both of these are not centered in SF. Notice that I'm not predicting the center moves to another city; rather, I imagine there being no clear center of gravity at all. There will be hotspots in cities like New York and London, but there will be a lesser need for people. Of course, many tech decision makers will still stay in SF, but I don't anticipate it will be as important for future founders to move there.

8. I am worried the public and AI companies aren't worried about the same issues. The public cares far more about data centers and where they are located, while AI companies are much more focused on model safety and x-risk. Both are valid and need to be appropriately addressed, though I believe the latter is far more important for humanity to get right. The relatively muted [reaction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=87DyyMV0kCY) to the OpenAI safety incident is a good example here. There needs to be better engagement and communication on the side of everyone involved; a lack of coordination and alignment can be dangerous.

9. I'm glad that we have companies like Pangram working on AI verification, and I believe we'll need more investment here in the future. However, we need better science on how performant such models can stay at small scales against adversarial inputs. At large scales, I'm confident in LLM watermarking playing a valuable role.

10. I believe we need more investment in post-AI futures too. We are nearing points where we can begin visualizing a lot of science getting solved, and it's worth thinking through that. The Anthropic Institute is a great example of this, and I hope we have more institutions and think tanks outside AI invested too. RAND-like spinoffs across the world are urgently needed.

11. AI safety organizations like AISI, METR, Epoch, and Redwood are doing incredibly mission-critical work, and it is incumbent on governments and labs to fund them more into the future. This looks like more compute and funding for researchers, as well as unrestricted frontier model access. I hope there are strong coalitions built here that can test frontier open model releases as well, especially those with dangerous capabilities.

12. Humanity's increasing split into subcommunities and echo chambers, which has been helped by large social networks, looks like it may be counteracted by AI. However, we must ensure that AI remains trusted as a public steward to help see this through, else we risk a worse future here. AIs that are completely aligned to current human views may serve to only further them, which can worsen echo chambers. A paternalistic approach won't work well, but we do need AI models to be grounded to express trusted viewpoints.
